How to Paraphrase Without Plagiarizing (5 Steps With Examples)

Paraphrasing means explaining someone else's idea in your own words. Done well, it shows your teacher you understood the source. Done badly, it's one of the most common causes of accidental plagiarism.

The good news is that there's a simple method that works every time.

What counts as plagiarism when you paraphrase

A paraphrase is plagiarism if:

  • You keep the original sentence structure and just swap in synonyms (often called "patchwriting"), or
  • You leave out the citation, even if every word is your own.

That second point surprises many students. The idea belongs to the author, so you must credit them even when the wording is completely yours.

A bad paraphrase vs a good one

Original source:

"Students who sleep less than seven hours a night are more likely to struggle with memory and concentration during exams."

Bad paraphrase (patchwriting):
"Learners who rest fewer than seven hours each night are more likely to have trouble with recall and focus during tests."

Every word has been swapped, but the structure is identical. A checker, or a teacher, will spot it. It's still the author's sentence in disguise.

Good paraphrase:
"Getting under seven hours of sleep can make it harder to remember material and stay focused in an exam (Author, Year)."

The idea is the same, the sentence is built differently, it's shorter, and it's cited.

The 5-step method

Step 1: Read until you understand it

Read the passage two or three times. If you can't explain it to a friend, you're not ready to paraphrase it. Look up words you don't know.

Step 2: Close the source

This is the most important step. If the original is in front of you, your brain copies its structure without meaning to.

Step 3: Write it from memory

Write the idea the way you'd explain it out loud. Start the sentence differently, change the order of ideas, and use your normal vocabulary. Shorter is fine. A paraphrase doesn't have to cover every detail.

Step 4: Compare with the original

Open the source again and check two things:

  • Is the meaning still accurate? You mustn't change what the author said.
  • Are there any runs of three or four identical words in a row? If so, rewrite that part, or put it in quotation marks if the exact words matter.

Step 5: Cite it

Add an in-text citation in the style your teacher wants (APA, MLA, Chicago...) and a matching entry in your reference list. Not sure how? See our guide on how to cite a website in APA 7.

One more worked example

Original source:

"Although renewable energy has become cheaper over the last decade, many developing countries still depend on coal because it is reliable and the infrastructure already exists."

Step 3, from memory: "Coal is still common in many developing countries. It's dependable and the plants are already built, even though renewables cost less than they used to."

Step 4, compare: The meaning is the same. The order is flipped (coal first, cost last). No long runs of identical words, apart from "developing countries", which is a normal term to keep.

Step 5, cite: "...cost less than they used to (Author, Year)."

Notice the paraphrase is a bit simpler than the original. That's fine. Simple and accurate beats fancy and copied.

Paraphrase, summarise or quote?

UseWhenNeeds citation?
ParaphraseYou need one specific idea, in about the same detailYes
SummaryYou need the main point of a long passage, much shorterYes
QuoteThe exact wording matters (a definition, a famous line)Yes, plus quotation marks

Most of your essay should be your analysis, with paraphrases and quotes as support, not the other way round.

Tricks that help

  • Change the starting point. If the source starts with the cause, start with the effect.
  • Switch active and passive. "Researchers found X" can become "X was the main finding."
  • Split or join sentences. Turn one long sentence into two short ones.
  • Keep technical terms. You don't need a synonym for "photosynthesis" or "inflation." Specialist terms are fine to keep.
  • Add your angle. Follow the paraphrase with what it means for your argument.

Check before you submit

When you're done, run the paragraphs where you used sources through a plagiarism checker. If a sentence is flagged, rewrite it from memory (Step 2 again) and make sure it's cited.

FAQ

Is paraphrasing plagiarism? No, as long as the wording and sentence structure are really yours and you cite the source of the idea. It becomes plagiarism if you only swap synonyms or leave out the citation.

Do I need to cite a paraphrase? Yes. The idea belongs to the original author, so a paraphrase always needs a citation, even if every word is your own.

How many words can be the same as the original? There is no official number, but a good rule is to avoid three or four identical words in a row. Keep technical terms, and quote any exact wording that matters.

Is it okay to use a paraphrasing tool? Using one to improve your own sentences is fine. Pasting someone else's text in and submitting the result is not, and the idea still needs a citation.
